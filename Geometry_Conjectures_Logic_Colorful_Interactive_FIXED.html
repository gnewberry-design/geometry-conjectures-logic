2. 📘 Lesson Guide / Overview

👥 Whole Group

Learning target: I can use patterns to make and test conjectures, identify counterexamples, and write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive of a conditional statement with at least 80% accuracy.

Why it matters: Mathematical reasoning helps us distinguish a pattern from a proven fact and evaluate whether statements are logically equivalent.

Success criteria: I can (1) propose a conjecture from examples, (2) disprove a false conjecture with one counterexample, (3) identify hypothesis and conclusion, and (4) correctly transform “If p, then q.”

Essential question: How do patterns and logical statements help us make valid mathematical arguments?

3. 🧰 Materials & Resources

👥 Whole Group
  • Notebook or digital notes; pencil; this interactive lesson
  • Optional: graph paper, whiteboard, and Canvas assignment
  • Vocabulary: conjecture, inductive reasoning, deductive reasoning, counterexample, conditional statement, hypothesis, conclusion, converse, inverse, contrapositive
Remember: Inductive reasoning uses observed patterns to make a conjecture. Deductive reasoning uses established facts or rules to reach a conclusion. A single counterexample disproves a universal claim.

4. 🔔 Warm-Up / Bell Ringer

👥 Whole Group

Look at the pattern: 3, 6, 12, 24, … What is the next number? What rule did you use?

Teacher prompt: Ask students whether observing four terms proves the rule must continue forever. Discuss why a conjecture needs justification.

5. 👩‍🏫 I DO – Direct Instruction (Learn)

👥 Whole Group

Part A: Conjectures

Observation: 5, 10, 15, 20

Conjecture: The next term is 25 because each observed term increases by 5.

Counterexample: “All prime numbers are odd” is false because 2 is prime and even.

Part B: Conditional Statements

Original: If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle.

Hypothesis (p): A figure is a square.

Conclusion (q): It is a rectangle.

📊 Reference Chart

StatementFormWhat changes?
Original conditionalIf p, then qNothing
ConverseIf q, then pSwitch
InverseIf not p, then not qNegate
ContrapositiveIf not q, then not pSwitch AND negate

Example using squares:

  • Converse: If a figure is a rectangle, then it is a square. (False)
  • Inverse: If a figure is not a square, then it is not a rectangle. (False)
  • Contrapositive: If a figure is not a rectangle, then it is not a square. (True)

⭐ A conditional statement and its contrapositive always have the same truth value. The converse and inverse always have the same truth value.

8. 🧩 Differentiation / Accommodations

👩‍🏫 Small Group

Extra Support

  • Highlight p and q in two different colors.
  • Use the reference chart while practicing.
  • Read the original statement aloud before rewriting.
  • For counterexamples, try small or unusual values first.

Extension

  • Create a true conditional with a false converse.
  • Explain why a conditional and its contrapositive are equivalent.
  • Write your own conjecture and test it with several examples.

Small-group reteach: Group students based on missed targets: patterns/counterexamples or conditional transformations. Model one new example, then reassess.
